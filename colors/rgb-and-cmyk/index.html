RGB and CMYK: why print never quite matches the screen

You design a poster on a computer in a brilliant blue. It comes back from the printer a little duller. Nothing went wrong. Screens and printers make colour in opposite ways, and each can show some colours that the other cannot.

RGB: colour from light

A screen is dark until it shines. It has tiny red, green and blue lights, and it makes colours by adding their light together. All three at full strength give white. This is additive colour, and its code is RGB.

CMYK: colour from ink

Paper is white until ink is put on it. A printer uses cyan, magenta and yellow inks, each of which absorbs one part of the light falling on the paper. The more ink, the darker the result. This is subtractive colour. A fourth ink, black, is added for deep blacks and sharp text. Its letter is K, for "key". Together they are CMYK.

How the two are linked

Each ink is the opposite of one light. Cyan ink absorbs red light, magenta absorbs green, and yellow absorbs blue. So where the screen has plenty of red, the printer uses little cyan, and so on.

Why colours shift

The range of colours a device can show is called its gamut. A screen can show very bright, saturated greens, blues and oranges that no mixture of four inks on paper can match. When such a colour is printed, the nearest printable colour is used, and it looks flatter. Deep, rich reds and many soft colours print well.

The paper matters too. Glossy coated paper keeps the ink on the surface and looks bright. Plain uncoated paper soaks it up and looks softer. And a screen glows, while paper only reflects the light of the room.

What the simple conversion can and cannot do

The CMYK figures on this site come from the plain arithmetic conversion: black is set by the brightest of the three lights, and the other inks by what remains. It is a fair starting point and is what most online tools show. A print shop does not use this formula. It uses a colour profile made for its own press, ink and paper, and its figures will differ. For any job that matters, ask the printer for a proof.

Practical advice for printing

  • Ask the printer which colour mode and profile they want, before you start.
  • Do not expect neon-bright screen colours on paper. Choose colours that are a little less saturated.
  • For black text, use black ink alone (0, 0, 0, 100), not a mixture, so that the letters stay sharp.
  • For brand colours that must match exactly, printers use ready-mixed "spot" inks.
  • Look at a printed proof in daylight before approving a large order.
