Warm and cool colours

Colours are often sorted into two groups. Reds, oranges and yellows are called warm, because they bring to mind fire and sunlight. Greens, blues and purples are called cool, like water, shade and the evening sky. The idea is simple, and it is one of the most useful in choosing colours for a room, a dress or a design.

Where the line falls

On the colour wheel the warm colours run from red through orange to yellow. The cool colours run from green through blue to violet. The two colours on the border, yellow-green and red-violet, can go either way depending on what is next to them.

These are habits of the eye and of custom, not laws. People differ, and so do cultures.

Every colour has a warm and a cool version

Warm and cool are also used inside a single colour. A red that leans towards orange, like tomato, is a warm red. A red that leans towards purple, like crimson, is a cool red. A yellowish green is warmer than a bluish green. Painters pay close attention to this, because mixing a warm red with a blue gives a muddy purple while a cool red gives a clean one.

Neutrals can be warm or cool too

Greys and whites are rarely perfectly neutral. A grey with a trace of blue is a cool grey. One with a trace of brown or yellow is a warm grey. Ivory and cream are warm whites. This is why two "white" paints can look so different on a wall.

Using them together

  • Mostly one, a little of the other. A cool blue room with a few orange cushions, or a warm poster with a small cool accent, has balance and a point of interest.
  • Warm for what should be noticed. Because warm colours come forward, a warm button on a cool page draws the eye.
  • Cool for large areas. Big backgrounds in strong warm colours tire the eye. Cool or neutral backgrounds are easier to look at for long.
  • Match the light. A room that faces north and gets cool light can take warmer colours. A hot, sunny room is helped by cooler ones.

Colour temperature of light

Light bulbs are also described as warm or cool. A "warm white" bulb gives a yellowish light, like a traditional filament bulb. A "cool daylight" bulb gives a bluish white. Confusingly, the number used for this, in kelvin, is lower for the warm bulbs and higher for the cool ones.
