Colour mixing: what two colours make?

What do red and blue make? It depends on what you are mixing. Paints behave one way, coloured lights another, and printing inks a third. This page gives the charts for all three and the reason they differ.

Mixing paints: red, yellow, blue

With paints, crayons and poster colours, the traditional primary colours are red, yellow and blue. They are called primary because they cannot be mixed from other paints. Mixing two of them gives a secondary colour.

+=
+=

Mixing a primary with the secondary next to it gives a tertiary colour, such as yellow-green or red-orange. Mixing all three primaries gives brown or a muddy grey, not black.

More paint mixtures

To makeMix
PinkAdd a little red to white. Always put the red into the white, a drop at a time, because a small amount of red goes a long way. A touch of yellow makes a warmer, salmon pink.
BrownMix all three primaries, red, yellow and blue, with rather more red and yellow than blue. Another way is to mix orange with a little blue, or red with green. Add white for tan and beige.
MaroonAdd a little black or dark brown to red. A touch of blue pushes it towards burgundy and wine.
Navy blueAdd a little black to blue. A trace of red or purple gives a deeper midnight blue.
TealMix blue and green in about equal parts and darken with a little black. White turns it into a soft sea green.
OliveMix yellow with a little black, which gives a surprisingly green olive, or mix green with a little red or brown and some yellow.
TurquoiseMix blue with a little green and plenty of white. More green moves it towards aquamarine.
CoralMix red and yellow to make orange, then add white and a little more red or pink.
LavenderAdd a little purple to plenty of white. A trace of blue keeps it cool.
GreyMix black and white. For a livelier grey, mix two opposite colours such as blue and orange with white. A trace of blue gives a cool grey, and a trace of brown a warm one.
BeigeAdd a very little brown to white, with a trace of yellow.
Sky blueAdd a little blue to plenty of white.

Mixing lights: red, green, blue

Screens, torches and stage lights add light to light. Their primaries are red, green and blue (RGB). Every colour on your phone is made from tiny red, green and blue dots.

+=
+=
+=

All three together give white. None gives black. This is called additive mixing, because each light adds brightness.

Mixing printing inks: cyan, magenta, yellow

A colour printer uses cyan, magenta and yellow inks, plus black (CMYK). Inks work by absorbing light, so each layer makes the paper darker. This is subtractive mixing.

+=
+=
+=

In theory all three inks make black. In practice they make a dark brown, so printers add a real black ink, the K.

Why paint and light disagree

A paint looks red because it soaks up most colours of light and sends back the red. Mix two paints and the mixture soaks up what both soak up, so less light comes back and the result is darker. Lights do the opposite: shine two together and more light reaches the eye. That is why red and green paints make brown while red and green lights make yellow.

The school primaries red, yellow and blue are a simple version of the printing primaries. Magenta is close to red and cyan is close to blue. That is why the school set works well for oranges and greens but gives dull purples.

Tints, shades and tones

  • Add white to a colour for a tint: red becomes pink.
  • Add black for a shade: red becomes maroon.
  • Add grey for a tone: the colour becomes softer and duller.

More on this on the tints, shades and tones page.

Tips for mixing paint

  • Add the darker colour to the lighter one, a little at a time. It takes a lot of white to lighten a dark mix.
  • Clean the brush between colours, or everything drifts towards brown.
  • To dull a colour without making it black, add a little of its opposite: a touch of green into red.
  • Mix enough for the whole job. Matching a mixed colour later is hard.

Each colour's own page, such as orange or brown, says how to mix it. See also the colour wheel.
