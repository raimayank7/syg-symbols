The colour wheel and colour harmony

A colour wheel sets the colours in a circle in the order of the rainbow, with red meeting violet to close the ring. It is the simplest tool for choosing colours that look good together. This page explains the wheel and the standard ways of picking colours from it.

What is on the wheel

  • Three primary colours, spaced evenly round the wheel. In the painter's wheel they are red, yellow and blue.
  • Three secondary colours between them, each made by mixing two primaries: orange, green and purple.
  • Six tertiary colours between those, with double names such as red-orange and blue-green.

The wheel on a computer is arranged by angle, called hue: red at 0°, yellow at 60°, green at 120°, cyan at 180°, blue at 240° and magenta at 300°. It is the same idea with the primaries of light. The wheel above is drawn this way.

Warm and cool halves

The reds, oranges and yellows are called warm. The greens, blues and purples are called cool. More on the warm and cool colours page.

Colour schemes from the wheel

  • Complementary: two colours directly opposite each other, such as blue and orange. They give the strongest contrast and make each other look brighter. Use one as the main colour and the other in small amounts.
  • Analogous: two or three neighbours, such as yellow, yellow-green and green. Calm and natural, like leaves or a sunset.
  • Triadic: three colours evenly spaced, such as red, yellow and blue. Lively, common in toys and children's designs.
  • Split complementary: a colour and the two neighbours of its opposite. Nearly as much contrast as complementary, but easier on the eye.
  • Tetradic or square: four colours evenly spaced. Rich, and best when one colour clearly leads.
  • Monochromatic: one colour in several tints and shades. Tidy and hard to get wrong.

The two wheels differ because their primaries differ. For screens and web design use the first column. For paints, clothes and rooms, most people use the second.

How to use a scheme well

The wheel gives hues, but a good scheme also varies lightness and strength. A pale tint of one colour beside a deep shade of its opposite is more pleasant than the two at full strength. A common guide is 60-30-10: one main colour for about 60% of what you see, a second for 30%, and an accent for 10%. Add white, grey or black as neutrals and they will not fight with anything.

A short history

Isaac Newton bent the spectrum of sunlight into a circle in his book Opticks in 1704, joining red to violet. Painters and printers have used wheels of one kind or another ever since.
