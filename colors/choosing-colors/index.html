How to choose colours that go together

Most people can tell when colours clash, but find it hard to choose colours that work. There is no need for a special gift. A few simple rules, borrowed from designers, are enough for a poster, a presentation, a website, a room or an outfit.

Rule 1: fewer colours

Use one main colour, one supporting colour and one accent. Three is plenty. Add white, black or grey as neutrals and they do not count. Most designs that look messy have too many colours, not the wrong ones.

Rule 2: the 60-30-10 guide

Give the main colour about 60% of the space, the supporting colour about 30% and the accent about 10%. In a room that might be the walls, the furniture and the cushions. On a web page: the background, the panels and headings, and the buttons. The small accent is where a bright colour belongs.

Rule 3: pick from the colour wheel

  • For calm: neighbours on the wheel (analogous), such as blue, blue-green and green.
  • For energy: opposites (complementary), such as blue with orange. Let one lead and keep the other small.
  • For safety: one colour in light and dark versions (monochromatic) with a neutral.

The palette maker does this for any starting colour, and the colour wheel page explains the schemes.

Rule 4: vary the lightness

Two colours of the same lightness sit uneasily together, even if their hues match well. Pair a light with a dark: pale blue with navy, cream with maroon. If you turned the design into a black and white photograph, you should still be able to tell the parts apart.

Rule 5: make text readable first

Whatever else you choose, text must stand out from its background. Dark text on a light background, or light on dark. Check doubtful pairs with the contrast checker. Never put red text on green or blue text on red.

Rule 6: borrow from what already works

Nature and tradition have done the testing. A peacock's blue, green and gold. The saffron, white and green of the national flag. A Kanjeevaram saree's maroon with gold. Sunset oranges with a deep blue sky. Take a photograph you like, pick three colours from it and use them.

Choosing for a purpose

  • A school chart or poster: a white or pale background, dark text, one bright colour for headings and one for highlights.
  • A presentation: the same two or three colours on every slide. Dark text on light slides is easiest to read in a bright room.
  • An invitation or card: a rich main colour with gold or cream, and plenty of empty space.
  • A shop sign: strong contrast above all, since it must be read from far away.
  • Clothes: one strong colour with neutrals, or two neighbours on the wheel. Repeat the accent once, in a dupatta, a tie or shoes.

Common mistakes

  • Using every colour at full strength. Soften most of them and keep one bright.
  • Putting two bright opposites side by side in equal amounts, which seems to flicker.
  • Choosing colours on a phone screen and expecting paint or print to match. Always test a small sample.
  • Forgetting people who cannot tell red from green. Add a label or a shape.
