Colour palette maker

Give it one colour. It works out the colours that go with it by six classic schemes. Tap any box to copy its code.

What the six schemes are

  • Complementary: your colour and the one opposite it on the colour wheel. Maximum contrast. Good for one thing that must stand out.
  • Analogous: your colour with the neighbour on each side. Quiet and harmonious, like the colours of a sunset or a forest.
  • Triadic: three colours a third of the wheel apart. Cheerful and balanced.
  • Split complementary: your colour with the two neighbours of its opposite. Lively, but easier to handle than a straight complementary pair.
  • Tetradic: four colours a quarter of the wheel apart. Plenty of variety, so pick one to lead.
  • Monochromatic: your colour alone, from pale tint to deep shade.

How to use a palette

A palette is a set of ingredients, not a recipe. Do not use the colours in equal amounts. Give one colour most of the space, a second a supporting part, and keep the brightest for small accents: buttons, headings, a border. Add white, a light grey or a very dark grey for backgrounds and text. If the colours feel too loud, make a tint or shade of each with the converter.

How the colours are worked out

Your colour is changed into hue, saturation and lightness. The tool then turns the hue by a fixed angle (180° for the opposite, 30° for a neighbour, 120° for a triad, 90° for a square) and keeps the saturation and lightness the same. This uses the colour wheel of screens, where the opposite of red is cyan. The painter's wheel, where the opposite of red is green, gives somewhat different partners.

Check before you use

Colours that suit each other may still be unreadable as text on one another. Test text and background pairs with the contrast checker. More advice is on the pages about the colour wheel and choosing colours. Nothing you type is sent anywhere.
